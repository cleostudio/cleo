The Moon looks like a decoration until you notice the receipts. Oceans rise and fall because a rock the size of a continent is continuously negotiating gravity with our planet. That negotiation has a side effect polite calendars ignore: Earth is gradually rotating more slowly, and the Moon is edging outward.

The theft is tiny. Milliseconds accumulate across centuries. Still, "day" is not a fixed coin. It is a relationship.

## Brake

Tidal bulges are dragged ahead of the Moon by Earth's spin and friction with basins and seafloors. The Moon tugs back on those bulges. Angular momentum moves around the system. Earth loses a little spin; the Moon gains a slightly higher, slower orbit.

Fossils and tidal rhythmites hint that days were shorter in the deep past - more of them per year when the Moon loomed closer. Some Devonian coral growth bands imply roughly 400 days in a year, which is another way of saying the clock ran faster when the Moon sat nearer. Mudstone laminations keep similar tallies. The beach is a history textbook if you know the dialect.

## Numbers

Laser ranging to reflectors left on the lunar surface measures the escape in centimeters per year - about the rate a fingernail grows. A few milliseconds of day-length change per century sounds like a rounding error until you remember that civilization's precision has caught up with geology's patience. Leap seconds, satellite orbits, and eclipse predictions all live in a world where Earth's rotation is a measured character, not a constant.

The Moon is not malicious. "Steals" is a joke with accurate bookkeeping.

## Stage

Without a large moon, Earth's obliquity might wander more wildly, complicating climate stability over long ages. With this Moon, we get eclipses that fit with eerie neatness, tides that sculpt coasts, and nights bright enough to mythologize. Culture noticed the drama early. Physics filled in the mechanism late.

The full Moon in a photograph looks still. Dynamically, it is a slow escapee - about a few centimeters farther from us each year, an orbital divorce conducted at glacial courtesy.

## Units

We name months after lunar habits even when our civil calendars refuse to stay synced. We plan launches and tides with software that knows the Moon's distance is not a poem. Meanwhile the same companion keeps rewriting the length of the day our languages treat as obvious.

That mismatch is useful. It reminds us that familiar units - day, month, year - are stories told by orbits, not commandments carved under the sky.

## Clock

The next time moonlight turns a street theatrical, enjoy the romance. Then, if you like, add the punchline: that light is delayed sunlight, and that orbit is the long receipt of tidal friction. The Moon that steals our days pays us back in tides, in eclipses, in a night sky with a face. It is the rare thief you thank for visiting - even as it quietly lengthens tomorrow.
