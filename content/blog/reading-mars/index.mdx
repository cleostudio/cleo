Mars is easy to over-promise. A field-guide reading stays smaller: a cold desert
world with polar ice, a thin carbon-dioxide air, and landscapes that once ran
with water. The day is nearly Earth-like; the year is nearly twice as long.
Seasons sharpen under a tilted axis and an eccentric orbit.

## Geography

Orientation begins with durable structure. The Tharsis bulge lifts a volcanic
province on a planetary scale. Olympus Mons rises as a shield volcano roughly
the width of France — the tallest known mountain in the Solar System. Beside
that rise, Valles Marineris opens as a canyon system thousands of kilometers
long, a tectonic scar rather than a river valley carved yesterday.

North and south do not match. Broad northern lowlands sit against older, cratered
southern highlands. That dichotomy is part of how you place any photograph: are
you looking at a young plain, an ancient highland, or the flank of Tharsis?

## Climate

Dust colors the sky. Polar layered deposits stack ice and dust that record
climate oscillations over millions of years. The air is thin; liquid water is
not a surface habit today. The wet chapter, if you want one, lives in rock —
minerals, sediments, and landforms that imply water earlier in the planet’s
story.

Companions stay modest: Phobos and Deimos are small, irregular moons, not ocean
worlds. For those, start with [Europa](/space/europa) or
[Enceladus](/space/enceladus) — or the comparison in
[Europa and Enceladus](/blog/icy-moons-europa-enceladus).

## How to read

Open the primer at [Mars](/space/mars). Notice scale before romance: a canyon
system, a volcano the size of a country, ice that remembers climate. When a
question wants fresher evidence or a comparison, ask [Cleo](/cleo) — and expect
a link home to the guide rather than a regenerated page.

Mars is often imagined as a second Earth. The shelf on Cleo treats it as a
smaller, drier sibling that still preserves an early wet chapter in rock — worth
returning to, not chasing every headline.
